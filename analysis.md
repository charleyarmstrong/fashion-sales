# Fashion Sales Dataset — Full Analysis

Source file: `fashion_sales.csv` (240 data rows + 1 header row, 14 columns).
Charts: `charts/` (generated by `make_charts.py`).
Scope: 24 months, January 2024 through December 2025.

---

## 1. What the dataset covers

A two-year sales history for a mid-market apparel business selling across **five product
categories** and **two sales channels**:

- **Categories:** Dresses, Denim, Outerwear, Footwear, Accessories
- **Channels:** Online, Store
- **Period:** 2024-01 … 2025-12 (24 months)
- **Totals:** 202,500 units sold, **$13,220,482 revenue**, $2,337,530 ad spend (blended ROAS 5.66)

It is a **monthly management report**, not transaction-level data. Every number is an
aggregate for one month, one category, one channel.

## 2. How it is organized (the grain)

Each row = **one month × one category × one channel**.

The grid is complete and perfectly balanced:

```
24 months × 5 categories × 2 channels = 240 rows
```

- No missing month/category/channel combinations, no duplicates.
- `(month, category, channel)` uniquely identifies every row.
- Because the panel is balanced, comparisons across categories or channels are
  apples-to-apples (each gets exactly 24 monthly observations).

Rows are ordered by month, then by a repeating category/channel block
(Dresses-Online, Dresses-Store, Denim-Online, …).

## 3. Column definitions

| Column | Meaning | Type | Observed values |
|---|---|---|---|
| `month` | Month of the aggregate | text `YYYY-MM` | 24 values, 2024-01 … 2025-12 |
| `year` | Calendar year (redundant with `month`) | int | 2024, 2025 |
| `month_num` | Month number (redundant with `month`) | int | 1–12 |
| `category` | Product category | text | Dresses, Denim, Outerwear, Footwear, Accessories |
| `channel` | Sales channel | text | Online, Store |
| `avg_temp_F` | Average temperature that month (°F) | float | 31.3 … 80.4 (24 distinct) |
| `list_price` | Nominal full retail price ($) | int | 35 / 70 / 85 / 110 / 180 (fixed per category) |
| `discount_pct` | Discount applied (%) | int | 0, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40 |
| `sale_price` | Realized average selling price ($) | float | 19.57 … 197.18 (240 distinct) |
| `ad_spend` | Advertising spend that month ($) | number | 2,050 … 19,270 |
| `avg_rating` | Average customer rating | float | 3.4 … 5.0 (15 distinct) |
| `units_sold` | Units sold | int | 220 … 2,488 |
| `revenue` | Revenue ($) | float | 17,257 … 154,512 |
| `return_rate` | Share of units returned (fraction) | float | 0.01 … 0.356 |

## 4. How the columns depend on each other

**Exact identities (hold for all 240 rows):**

- **`revenue = sale_price × units_sold`** — verified to floating-point precision
  (max absolute error ≈ 3×10⁻¹¹). Revenue is fully derived; it adds no independent
  information.
- **`year` and `month_num` are derived from `month`** — pure redundancy.
- **`list_price` is derived from `category`** — it is a constant per category and
  never varies within one:

  | Category | list_price |
  |---|---:|
  | Accessories | $35 |
  | Denim | $70 |
  | Dresses | $85 |
  | Footwear | $110 |
  | Outerwear | $180 |

- **`avg_temp_F` is a month-level value** repeated across all 10 rows of that month
  (24 distinct values, constant within each month). It carries no within-month variation.

**Relationships that are *not* exact — the surprising part:**

- **`sale_price` is *not* `list_price × (1 − discount_pct/100)`.** In **all 240 rows**
  the two disagree. The gap ranges from **−$15.78 to +$17.18** (mean −$0.19, std $5.06).
  Realized price averages **77.3% of list** (range 54.1%–109.5%).

  This means `sale_price` is effectively an **independent realized-price column**, and
  `list_price`/`discount_pct` should be read as *nominal* references only. Any margin or
  "expected price" math must use `sale_price`, not `list_price` and `discount_pct`.

**Everything else is free variation:** `ad_spend`, `avg_rating`, `units_sold`,
`sale_price`, `return_rate` are not mechanically linked to the other columns. This is the
set of variables that the analysis below relates to one another.

## 5. Data quality and things that are surprising

1. **No missing values** (all 3,360 cells populated) and **no duplicate rows**.
2. **Some items sell above list price.** In **21 of 240 rows** `sale_price > list_price`
   — and every one of them is a **0% discount** row (e.g. 2024-04 Outerwear Online:
   list $180, "0% discount", sale price **$191.11**). All 43 zero-discount rows have a
   sale price different from list. So "0% discount" does not mean "sold at list".
3. **`avg_temp_F` is a monthly figure, not a per-row measurement.** Treating rows as
   independent observations overstates statistical confidence in temperature effects.
4. **Ratings barely vary** — std 0.282 over a 3.4–5.0 range; the category spread is only
   0.054. They behave more like a channel-quality flag than a product differentiator.
5. **Discounting is structural, not promotional** — the median discount is 25%, only
   43/240 rows (18%) sell at 0% off, and the average discount barely moved year over year
   (22.9% → 22.3%).
6. **Ad spend is almost flat across categories** (≈$9.4k–$10.2k per row) even though
   return on that spend varies 2.8×–8.5× between them.
7. **Minor cosmetic formatting:** line 161 writes `avg_rating` as `5` (not `5.0`) and
   `ad_spend` as `8770.0`; some `avg_temp_F`/`avg_rating` values appear as `67.0`, `4.0`.
   Numerically harmless.
8. The dataset has the fingerprints of a **synthetic/generated** table (clean panel,
   formula-consistent revenue, no missing data, engineered-looking relationships).

## 6. Seasonal patterns

**One dominant peak: November–December.**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly revenue range | $453,369 (2024-06) → $754,943 (2025-12) |
| Mean month | $550,853 |
| Seasonality index, Nov / Dec | **130.5 / 132.1** |
| All other months | 88.7 – 98.9 |
| Nov+Dec mean vs other months | $723,455 vs $516,333 = **+40.1%** |
| Nov+Dec share of annual revenue | 22.4% (2024), 21.4% (2025) |

The seasonality is **category-driven**, with two distinct seasons:

| Category | Peak month (avg units) | Trough month | Peak/trough |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Dresses | Jul — 2,160 | Feb — 479 | **4.51×** |
| Outerwear | Dec — 1,243 | Jun — 312 | **3.99×** |
| Footwear | Dec — 1,185 | Apr — 497 | 2.39× |
| Denim | Dec — 1,184 | Apr — 639 | 1.85× |
| Accessories | Dec — 1,432 | Feb — 811 | 1.77× |

- Summer = Dresses surge (July peak); Winter = Outerwear/Footwear/Denim/Accessories surge
  (December peak).
- Accessories is the least seasonal (1.77× swing).
- Revenue is **not concentrated**: the top 5 month-category rows are only 5.5% of total
  revenue.

## 7. 2024 vs 2025

| Metric | 2024 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | $6,406,734 | $6,813,748 | **+6.4%** |
| Units sold | 97,712 | 104,788 | **+7.2%** |
| Avg order value | $65.57 | $65.02 | −0.8% |
| Ad spend | $1,098,180 | $1,239,350 | **+12.9%** |
| ROAS | 5.83 | 5.50 | **−5.8%** |
| Return rate (unit-wtd) | 0.172 | 0.179 | +4.2% |
| Avg rating (unit-wtd) | 4.262 | 4.209 | −1.2% |
| Avg discount | 22.9% | 22.3% | −2.7% |

Findings:

1. **Growth is volume-led, not price-led.** Units (+7.2%) outgrew revenue (+6.4%) while
   AOV slipped 0.8%.
2. **Ad efficiency is deteriorating.** Spend rose 12.9% for 6.4% more revenue; ROAS fell
   from 5.83 to 5.50.
3. **Category mix is essentially frozen.** Every category grew 5–9% (Dresses +8.7%,
   Accessories +6.7%, Denim +6.1%, Outerwear +5.5%, Footwear +5.1%); revenue shares moved
   <0.5pp. Units: Accessories +9.0%, Dresses +8.9%, Denim +8.5%, Footwear +4.5%,
   Outerwear +3.4%.
4. **Channels diverged:** Online +10.0% vs Store +2.4%. Online rose from 51.8% to
   **53.6%** of revenue — toward the channel with the higher return rate.

## 8. How temperature affects each category

Month-level analysis (N = 24 per category), because `avg_temp_F` only varies by month:

| Category | r(units, temp) | r(revenue, temp) | Slope (units/°F) | Warm-half units | Cold-half units |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Dresses | **+0.68** | **+0.85** | +18.6 | 2,410 | 1,570 |
| Outerwear | **−0.82** | **−0.89** | −16.3 | 851 | 1,808 |
| Denim | −0.35 | −0.30 | −4.4 | 1,512 | 1,820 |
| Accessories | −0.30 | −0.34 | −3.9 | 1,891 | 2,199 |
| Footwear | −0.26 | −0.07 | −3.2 | 1,292 | 1,522 |

- **Dresses and Outerwear are strongly, oppositely weather-driven.**
- **Footwear is weather-neutral** (r = −0.07 on revenue).
- The naive row-level correlation across all rows is only **−0.08**, because it blends
  opposite-signed categories; aggregate by category before drawing conclusions.

## 9. Do bigger discounts lead to more units sold?

**Yes — one of the strongest relationships in the data.** Overall Pearson r = **+0.513**,
Spearman +0.52.

| Discount | Mean units | n |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0% | 586 | 43 |
| 10% | 607 | 12 |
| 15% | 812 | 13 |
| 20% | 784 | 45 |
| 25% | 802 | 28 |
| 30% | 911 | 46 |
| 40% | **1,129** | 53 |

Units roughly **double from 0% to 40% off** (+93%). Robustness:

- **Within every category:** r = +0.55 (Dresses), +0.57 (Outerwear), +0.55 (Accessories),
  +0.69 (Footwear), +0.75 (Denim).
- **Within every month:** mean r = +0.415, positive in **22 of 24 months**.
- **Two-way fixed effects** (removing month and category means): within-r = **+0.476**.
- Consistent with price: two-way within-r(units, `sale_price`) = **−0.445**.
- Confounding is minimal: r(discount, temp) = −0.14, r(discount, month) = 0.09.

*Caveat:* this is an association. Discounts are chosen, presumably to move product, so
causality cannot be proven from the data alone.

## 10. Does ad spending pay off?

**Yes on average, but very unevenly, and the marginal signal is weak.**

- Row-level r(ad, revenue) = **+0.208**; r(ad, units) = +0.252.
- OLS slope ≈ **$1.23 revenue per $1 of ad spend**; blended **ROAS = 5.66**.

| Category | Revenue | Ad spend | ROAS |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Outerwear | $4,045,992 | $474,890 | **8.52** |
| Dresses | $2,995,076 | $456,100 | 6.57 |
| Footwear | $2,794,483 | $452,080 | 6.18 |
| Denim | $2,078,650 | $489,100 | 4.25 |
| Accessories | $1,306,281 | $465,360 | **2.81** |

| Channel | ROAS |
|---|---:|
| Store | **6.27** |
| Online | 5.20 |

Category × channel ROAS ranges **4.1×** — best Outerwear-Store (10.52), worst
Accessories-Online (2.54) — while spend per row is nearly flat across categories
(Accessories $9,695 vs Denim $10,190).

Two cautions:

- **Month-level r(spend, revenue) = +0.012** (N = 24): higher spend in a month does not
  coincide with higher revenue in that month.
- Within-category correlations are modest: Footwear +0.61, Denim +0.56, Dresses +0.37,
  Accessories +0.38, Outerwear +0.18.

So ads pay back overall, but the data does not demonstrate that *increasing* spend
generates *incremental* revenue. The case for reallocating budget rests on the ROAS gaps.

## 11. What relates to returns?

Overall unit-weighted return rate = **0.176** (≈1 in 5.7 units).

**Channel is the strongest factor — r(return, Online) = +0.851:**

| Channel | Return rate |
|---|---:|
| Online | **0.246** |
| Store | 0.099 |

The gap holds inside every category:

| Category | Online | Store |
|---|---:|---:|
| Footwear | 0.297 | 0.147 |
| Dresses | 0.289 | 0.149 |
| Denim | 0.223 | 0.066 |
| Accessories | 0.212 | 0.065 |
| Outerwear | 0.211 | 0.070 |

- **Category:** Dresses and Footwear tie at 0.224 vs Denim 0.147, Outerwear 0.144,
  Accessories 0.141.
- **Ratings:** r(return, rating) = **−0.353**, holding within channel (Online −0.27,
  Store −0.29). By rating bucket: ≤4.0 → 0.219; 4.1–4.3 → 0.179; ≥4.4 → 0.139.
- **No seasonality:** r(return, temp) = −0.004; Q4 (0.176) = rest of year (0.176);
  monthly range only 0.150–0.193.
- **No discount/price effect:** r(discount) = −0.10, r(sale_price) = +0.05,
  r(list_price) = +0.02.
- The apparent r(return, ad_spend) = +0.228 is a **channel artifact** (Online has both
  higher spend, $11,168 vs $8,311 per row, and higher returns); within channel it
  disappears (−0.127 Online, −0.006 Store).

## 12. What relates to ratings?

Ratings barely move: std 0.282, range 3.4–5.0, mean 4.23.

| Factor | r with rating |
|---|---:|
| Return rate | **−0.353** |
| Units sold | +0.132 |
| Ad spend | −0.085 |
| Discount | −0.013 |
| Sale price | −0.014 |
| Temperature | +0.004 |

- By category: Accessories 4.253, Dresses 4.249, Outerwear 4.248, Footwear 4.215,
  Denim 4.199 — a spread of only 0.054.
- **By channel: Store 4.311 vs Online 4.164** (+0.147) — the only meaningful gap.

Ratings are effectively a weak proxy for the return experience and a small channel
signal; they do not differentiate products, prices, or seasons.

## 13. Summary of findings

1. **Strong, category-specific seasonality.** Nov–Dec runs +40% above average; Dresses
   peak in July (4.5× their trough), Outerwear in December (4.0×). No return-rate
   seasonality.
2. **2025 grew 6.4% on volume (+7.2% units), but efficiency slipped.** Ad spend +12.9%
   for +6.4% revenue (ROAS 5.83 → 5.50); Online's revenue share rose to 53.6%.
3. **Temperature drives demand in exactly two categories:** Dresses (+0.68) and Outerwear
   (−0.82); Footwear is weather-neutral.
4. **Discounts and units move together robustly** (overall r = +0.51; within month+category
   r = +0.48), roughly doubling units from 0% to 40% off.
5. **Ads pay back 5.66× on average but 2.81×–8.52× by category**, with nearly flat budget
   allocation — the clearest reallocation opportunity.
6. **Returns are driven by channel (Online 24.6% vs Store 9.9%) and category
   (Dresses/Footwear 22.4%)**, and tracked by ratings (−0.35); not by price, discount, or
   season.
7. **Ratings are uninformative** except as a weak return proxy and a small channel gap.

## 14. Method, caveats, and reproduction

**Caveats**

- The data looks **synthetically generated**: a perfectly balanced panel, formula-exact
  revenue, no missing values, and engineered-looking relationships.
- `sale_price` does **not** reconcile with `list_price × (1 − discount_pct)` in any row;
  treat `sale_price` as authoritative and `list_price`/`discount_pct` as nominal.
- `avg_temp_F` is a monthly value repeated across rows; temperature analyses are done at
  the month level (N = 24) to avoid pseudo-replication.
- All relationships are **associations**. Price, discount, and ad spend are chosen, not
  randomly assigned, so none of the findings establish causality.
